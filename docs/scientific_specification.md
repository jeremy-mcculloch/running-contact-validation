# Scientific Specification: Running Contact Validation

**Status:** Draft 1.4 — FEniCSx/DOLFINx, Green–Lagrange-rate state-free viscosity, and regularized penalty normal contact approved for the initial implementation. Detailed friction, beam coupling, time integration, and full-system periodic solving remain provisional.
**Purpose:** Predict ground reaction forces (GRFs) from measured internal human kinematics and an independently parameterized shoe–ground model, then validate against withheld force-plate measurements.

## 1. Scientific objective and independence

The prediction pipeline uses prescribed joint kinematics, body inertial properties, running speed, ground geometry, and independently established shoe parameters. Ground reaction force and moment measurements are **not** used for prediction, parameter tuning, initialization, gait-event enforcement, or periodic-shooting optimization on held-out validation trials. Measured GRFs, moments and center of pressure (COP) are used only for evaluation. Experimental kinematics may be used to initialize a separately reported short-horizon diagnostic, but not as force input.

Two validation modes are planned: (A) periodic-orbit prediction with unknown global initial state estimated from periodicity and speed; (B) short-horizon forward prediction initialized from independently measured kinematics. Report both predicted forces and disagreement between predicted and measured global motion.

## 2. Scope and staged fidelity

- **Approved:** Begin with 2D sagittal-plane mechanics and design interfaces for a later 3D extension. The 2D model cannot validate mediolateral forces or 3D contact moments.
- Rigid anatomical segments with prescribed joint-angle trajectories; no muscle activation, metabolic, or internal biological dissipation model.
- Begin with a synthetic body and Hunt–Crossley-like unilateral compliant contact; replace with the independently parameterized carbon-plated shoe model.
- **Approved:** Use a full-body rigid-segment representation including both legs, trunk, head, and arms; retain subject-specific mass and inertia bookkeeping.
- **Approved:** Support both stationary overground and treadmill trials. Begin with flat stationary-ground verification, then add a moving-belt ground boundary condition. Specify lab-frame versus belt-frame speed, forces, contact velocity, and periodicity for each experiment.

## 3. Frames, units and sign conventions

Use SI units. World x is forward, world y is upward, and positive sagittal rotation is counterclockwise. Gravity is **g** = (0, -9.80665) m/s² unless site-specific values are justified. Define the body COM over all included body and shoe masses **exactly once**. All forces are external ground-on-system forces; all moments are taken about the instantaneous whole-system COM. Clearly distinguish anatomical foot, shoe upper interface, deformable shoe, and outsole-ground contact.

## 4. State and prescribed kinematics

For a planar model without shoe internal states, use

z = [x_COM, y_COM, v_x, v_y, phi, H_COM].

Joint angles theta(t) and angular velocities theta_dot(t) are prescribed from a smooth, periodic fit to mocap. Do not require joint angular accelerations in the global state equations. **Approved:** The final shoe model is a finite-element model with distributed dynamic deformation. Augment the state with its independent deformation coordinates and velocities (and any viscoelastic internal variables); impose appropriate periodicity on physical shoe states, accounting for foot exchange and translation symmetry. Do not assume a single low-dimensional state vector will suffice. Inertial body configuration, effective moment of inertia I_eff(theta), and shape angular momentum h(theta,theta_dot) come from a consistent rigid multibody model.

H_COM = I_eff(theta) * phi_dot + h(theta, theta_dot)

so phi_dot = (H_COM - h)/I_eff. COM-relative pelvis and foot transforms are recomputed from theta and phi at each solver evaluation. Foot contact-point velocities must include v_COM, phi_dot, and theta_dot. Do not impose instantaneous COM position corrections.

## 5. Global dynamics

x_dot = v_x; y_dot = v_y

v_x_dot = F_ground,x / m

v_y_dot = F_ground,y / m - g

phi_dot = (H_COM - h(theta,theta_dot)) / I_eff(theta)

H_COM_dot = M_ground,COM

Here m is the total mass of the modeled system and ground contact moments include lever arms and any actual contact couples. All contact forces are generated by the shoe model. Prescribed joint motion may require actuator work; no joint-work or internal dissipation constraint is imposed. Report physically implausible inferred joint torques as a later diagnostic, not as a prediction input.

**Critical mechanics requirement (approved FE scope; implementation unresolved):** The six-state COM/angular-momentum equations are exact only when COM and H refer to the entire modeled moving system and all relevant internal momentum is included. Distributed shoe deformation can carry mass and angular momentum. Decide whether the final solver (a) couples a full-body floating-base multibody model to shoe deformation DOFs, (b) uses a rigorously derived reduced-order partition with shoe momentum terms, or (c) justifies neglecting these terms by convergence/sensitivity tests. Do not simply append shoe states to the six-state equations without deriving consistent total momentum and interface reactions.

## 6. Contact model

The baseline uses unilateral compliant normal contact with a Hunt–Crossley-type law, smooth only where justified for optimization. Normal force must not become tensile. Tangential contact requires a defined friction/slip law and consistent treatment of sticking, sliding, and separation. The final shoe model replaces the baseline without changing the body-dynamics interface. Do not enforce zero velocity for all outsole points: only material points currently sticking to a stationary ground surface have zero relative tangential velocity. Normal nonpenetration and tangential friction are separate conditions.

**Approved:** The final shoe is a finite-element (FE) model with distributed dynamic deformation, not merely an algebraic force–displacement law. Initially assume a rigid anatomical foot–shoe attachment. The coupling interface must exchange foot attachment pose/velocity and interface reaction wrenches; return ground forces/moments and shoe deformation states or their residual equations, plus energy/contact diagnostics. The coupled system must account for shoe inertia, constraints, and foot–shoe reaction forces consistently. Solid foam elements and beam plate elements are approved; their constitutive laws, discretization details, mesh resolution, contact algorithm, friction law, time integration, and coupling strategy remain **unresolved**. FE formulation should admit mass and internal damping, distinguish recoverable strain energy from dissipation, and define contact with the ground separately from the rigid anatomical foot attachment.

## 7. Periodic-orbit formulation

**Approved:** Use a scaled least-squares periodicity objective and report individual residuals rather than enforcing a perfect orbit at any cost. For a full stride duration T at independently measured mean forward speed V, require approximately:

x(T)-x(0) = V*T

y(T)-y(0) = 0

v_x(T)-v_x(0) = 0

v_y(T)-v_y(0) = 0

phi(T)-phi(0) = 0 modulo a prescribed net rotation (zero for normal steady running)

H(T)-H(0) = 0

and appropriate periodicity of independent shoe deformation and internal states, including correct left/right labeling, material-point mapping, and any stride-to-stride translation symmetry. Fix x(0)=0 because horizontal translation is a gauge freedom on homogeneous flat ground. **Treadmill caveat:** establish a consistent belt/laboratory frame. For steady treadmill running, lab-frame COM displacement over a stride is approximately zero, not V*T; belt speed enters the contact law and speed constraint. Do not reuse overground periodic boundary conditions unchanged. The five rigid-body initial unknowns are y0, vx0, vy0, phi0 and H0. Six global residual conditions generally form an overdetermined problem when speed is prescribed. Minimize scaled residuals, report each component and its units, and do not force agreement by modifying shoe parameters or injecting unexplained forces. Existence and uniqueness are not guaranteed. Test multiple initial guesses and, if needed, multiple shooting or collocation.

The joint trajectory itself must be periodic and sufficiently differentiable; a complete stride is preferable to a single step unless bilateral symmetry is explicitly imposed. Report distortion introduced by smoothing and periodic fitting.

## 8. Numerical verification

Develop in increasing complexity: (1) ballistic COM and angular momentum conservation in flight; (2) rigid-body and internal-shape angular momentum benchmarks; (3) isolated Hunt–Crossley contact and passive energy tests; (4) multiple contact points and moments; (5) coupled integration and time-step/tolerance convergence; (6) synthetic periodic orbit with known solution; (7) real joint trajectories; (8) detailed shoe mechanics and convergence. Numerical failure must be distinguishable from physically incompatible prescribed gait.

Do not add force clipping, acceleration clipping, position projection, arbitrary stabilization, or hidden regularization that changes the model without documented scientific approval.

## 9. Data requirements

**Development:** synthetic kinematics and generic body/shoe parameters; optional public running mocap.

**Final validation:** both overground and treadmill protocols, full-body bilateral mocap (including pelvis and arms/trunk), subject mass/height and scaling data, synchronized force plates with 3D forces and moments, running speed and full-stride timing, shoe identity/size/geometry/mass, shoe material characterization, ground properties, foot-to-shoe registration, and experimental metadata. Retain force-plate data in a separate validation-only data path. Prefer multiple subjects, speeds, and held-out trials. Measure pelvis orientation and global motion even if not prescribed, to test predicted global kinematics.

## 10. Validation outputs

Report horizontal/vertical GRF waveforms, peaks, impulses, loading rates, contact timing, ground-contact moments, COP where identifiable, pelvis orientation, COM trajectory, periodicity residuals, and sensitivity to model parameters, kinematic smoothing and numerical resolution. For multi-contact trials, distinguish net force from per-foot force; net COM acceleration alone cannot uniquely separate bilateral contacts. Pre-register filtering, alignment, metrics, and held-out datasets before inspecting validation outcomes.

### FE coupling architecture (provisional recommendation, not approved)

Use a **partitioned software architecture** with three interfaces: (i) full-body rigid multibody kinematics and inertia, (ii) FE shoe deformation and foot attachment, and (iii) unilateral ground contact and friction. The first production FE coupling may be monolithic (one nonlinear residual solve) or strongly coupled partitioned (iterated subsolves); this choice requires benchmarks. Do not assume one-way coupling from body to shoe, since shoe reaction forces must update body motion. An explicit weakly coupled staggered scheme is a verification baseline only unless stability and momentum/energy errors are demonstrated acceptable.

For FE nodal displacements u and velocities u_dot, a generic semidiscrete shoe balance is

M_FE(u) u_ddot + f_int(u, u_dot, a) + f_damp(u, u_dot, a) = f_contact(u, u_dot) + f_attach(u, body_state, theta),

with additional material internal variables a as needed. This is schematic: the actual form depends on the choice of material formulation, finite-strain kinematics, attachment constraints, and contact enforcement. Attachment reactions must be equal and opposite on the body and shoe. The overall translational and angular momentum balances must include both rigid anatomical segments and all deformable FE mass. A body-only COM is not a substitute for the total-system COM.

A safe initial implementation sequence is: a single deformable bar/beam with known modes; a small FE pad pressed against rigid ground; a prescribed-motion rigid attachment; coupled rigid-body/FE bouncing; then bilateral shoe contact and periodic running. Use mesh, time-step, momentum, and energy convergence tests before incorporating the carbon-plate shoe geometry.

### Explicit assumptions to verify

- Rigid anatomical segments adequately represent full-body mass distribution; soft-tissue artifact affects kinematic estimates but is not directly simulated.
- Joint trajectories are prescribed and are not corrected in response to simulated contact loads; muscles provide whatever joint torques are required, subject to post hoc plausibility checks.
- A complete stride can be approximated as periodic; individual stride variability and bilateral asymmetry are reported rather than silently removed.
- Ground is initially flat; overground ground velocity is zero, while treadmill ground velocity is nonzero in the laboratory frame.
- Contact forces are generated by independently parameterized shoe mechanics, not force plates, force-derived gait events, or hidden stabilizers.
- Rigid foot–shoe attachment is an initial simplification, not a claim that all real shoe/foot relative motion is negligible.
- The baseline Hunt–Crossley model is a software benchmark, not a physically validated carbon-plated shoe model.
- For distributed shoe dynamics, treating the whole-body rigid-segment COM as the total COM is only valid after accounting for or bounding the shoe's deformable mass and momentum.
- Model predictions may fail to satisfy all periodic constraints; residuals are scientific outputs and must not be hidden.

## 14. FE architecture decision update (draft 0.4)

### 14.1 Confirmed design intent

- **D23 APPROVED:** Foam is a continuum solid, represented by finite elements with distributed mass and deformation.
- **D24 APPROVED:** Carbon plate is initially a 2D beam discretization, coupled mechanically to the foam. A 3D plate/shell formulation is a later design decision, not yet approved.
- **D25 WORKING INTERPRETATION:** "New" FE software means build a new project around a suitable existing open-source FE framework, rather than use an already-developed proprietary shoe model or implement a general FE engine from scratch. Confirm this interpretation before committing to a solver.

### 14.2 Recommended software stack (PROVISIONAL; not approved)

- **First candidate: FEniCSx + PETSc** for nonlinear solid mechanics, variational residuals, sparse Jacobians, and implicit solution of the foam FE equations. This is a *candidate*, not a verified drop-in solution for frictional contact or mixed-dimensional beam-to-solid coupling. Those features may require custom variational terms, specialized libraries, or additional implementation work.
- **Beam module:** implement/test a small 2D geometrically appropriate beam formulation, or integrate a compatible beam package after a prototype confirms coupling and boundary conditions. Verify axial/bending modes, interface force transfer, and finite-rotation assumptions. Do not assume a generic solid FE library directly supports the desired beam coupling.
- **Biomechanics:** OpenSim for anatomical kinematics and rigid-body mass/inertia extraction, wrapped behind an interface; avoid making the FE solver depend on OpenSim at every nonlinear residual evaluation if quantities can be cached or evaluated efficiently.
- **Orchestration:** Python with NumPy/SciPy and pytest; PETSc-backed nonlinear/linear solvers where appropriate. Pin versions and keep core analytic tests independent of OpenSim/FEniCSx.
- **Alternative candidate:** SfePy or another open-source FE library if a short proof-of-concept demonstrates substantially simpler implementation of the necessary solid/beam/contact combination. Make the final choice based on a benchmark, not popularity alone.

### 14.3 Coupled equations and mass accounting (REQUIRED)

A schematic coupled generalized-coordinate system is

    M(q,u) [q_ddot; u_ddot] + r_internal(q,u,q_dot,u_dot,a)
      + G(q,u)^T lambda_attach
      = f_gravity + f_ground + f_prescribed_motion,

where q represents independent floating-body coordinates, u represents FE deformation coordinates after removal of redundant rigid motions, a represents constitutive internal variables, and lambda_attach represents attachment reactions when constraints are enforced with multipliers. This is schematic only: prescribed joint coordinates must be eliminated or consistently constrained, and signs/constraint terms must be derived for the chosen discretization. Ground contact adds unilateral/frictional conditions or a documented regularization.

**Do not integrate the original six-state COM system independently of FE mass** and then append shoe dynamics. Two mathematically consistent implementation routes are permissible: (A) floating-base rigid multibody plus FE coordinates, with whole-system COM and H computed diagnostically; (B) a rigorously derived total-system COM/angular-momentum formulation with internal FE momentum and shape contributions. Route A is the **provisional recommendation** for the first fully coupled implementation because interface forces and constraints can be represented explicitly. Verify whole-system linear and angular momentum balances in either case.

### 14.4 Contact and integration (PROVISIONAL)

- Initially use a stationary rigid flat plane; later set the ground's tangential velocity to the treadmill belt speed in the laboratory frame.
- Model normal unilateral contact and tangential friction at outsole surface quadrature points or contact nodes. A regularized penalty law is acceptable for early verification; the final normal-contact algorithm (penalty, augmented Lagrangian, or complementarity) and friction law require independent approval and convergence tests.
- Start with implicit Newmark or generalized-alpha integration for the FE/rigid coupled system, with consistent interface constraints and nonlinear iterations. Verify numerical damping does not masquerade as shoe material dissipation. Explicit integration remains an alternative if stability limits and cost are favorable.
- Favor a monolithic nonlinear residual or a strongly coupled partitioned scheme with interface iterations. One-pass force exchange is not accepted for final results without demonstrated stability and conservation.
- A material-point sticking condition means zero **relative tangential velocity** to the ground; do not enforce zero velocity on the anatomical foot or all outsole points.

### 14.5 2D interpretation (SUPERSEDED BY SECTION 15)

A 2D solid continuum generally predicts forces **per unit out-of-plane thickness**. Before using its output as whole-shoe force, choose plane strain, plane stress, or a reduced effective-width approximation, and specify the physical width/integration rule. The foam's 2D solid model and plate's 2D beam model must use consistent thickness, mass, and force units. A sagittal slice is not automatically a quantitatively accurate model of a 3D shoe.

### 14.6 FE implementation gates for coding agents

1. **FE environment proof-of-concept:** install pinned framework; solve a linear elastic bar/rectangle with known displacement and force; verify mesh refinement.
2. **Dynamic solid:** free vibration of a small elastic body; verify modal frequency, total momentum and energy behavior under time refinement.
3. **Nonlinear foam:** finite-strain compression/unloading of a block; verify constitutive consistency, tangent/Jacobian, and positive material dissipation.
4. **Carbon beam:** static cantilever deflection and free vibration; verify beam mass and bending stiffness.
5. **Solid–beam coupling:** impose known displacements; verify interface continuity and equal-and-opposite reactions, including torque balance.
6. **Ground contact:** compression, release, stick/slip and moving belt; verify contact inequalities, frictional dissipation and convergence.
7. **Floating body–shoe coupling:** drop/bounce a rigid mass attached to FE shoe; verify total-system momentum, energy balance and interface work.
8. **Full prescribed-kinematics runner:** introduce full-body OpenSim adapter and bilateral FE shoes; test gait periodicity, computational cost, and sensitivity before force-plate validation.

The FE model may be reduced for initial periodic-orbit discovery, but the final reported GRFs must be computed from the approved full model or from a separately validated reduced model with quantified error. Keep measured GRFs inaccessible to the prediction/optimization modules.

## 15. Effective-width sagittal FE model — decision update (draft 0.5)

### 15.1 Approved decision and its limits

**APPROVED by user:** The initial continuum foam FE model is a two-dimensional sagittal section with an **effective out-of-plane width**, followed by eventual 3D extension. This is a dimensional-reduction strategy, **not** approval of a particular plane-stress/plane-strain constitutive assumption, numerical width, or calibration method. The final FE architecture remains distributed dynamic solid foam plus a 2D beam carbon plate; both treadmill and overground running remain in scope.

### 15.2 Mathematical convention (historical constant-width benchmark; superseded for final model by Section 16)

Let `f_2D` and `m_2D` denote the integrated in-plane force and sagittal moment **per unit out-of-plane width**, respectively, for a 2D continuum formulation. For a constant independently specified effective width `b_eff` in meters:

    F_total = b_eff * f_2D
    M_total = b_eff * m_2D
    M_foam_total = b_eff * M_foam_per_width
    K_foam_total = b_eff * K_foam_per_width

Apply the same width to continuum mass, inertia, internal forces, damping, external loading, and contact traction integration. If the FE package accepts a thickness parameter, **use that thickness once**; do not multiply assembled outputs by `b_eff` again. Track units explicitly in tests: N/m times m gives N; kg/m times m gives kg; N (moment per width) times m gives N·m.

A uniform `b_eff` is retained ONLY as a numerical benchmark. The approved production 2D model uses longitudinally varying `b_eff(X)` in the reference configuration, with consistent mass, stiffness, and load integration as defined in Section 16. Do not fit width to held-out GRFs. Establish it from independent geometry or mechanical characterization, or propagate a prespecified uncertainty range.

The carbon plate's 2D beam is a separate one-dimensional structural representation. Specify whether its section properties (`EA`, `EI`, mass per length) describe the **whole plate** or are **per unit width**. If whole-plate properties are used, **do not multiply by foam effective width**. Its bond to the foam must transmit equal-and-opposite forces and moments without double-counting mass or stiffness.

### 15.3 Out-of-plane material assumption (UNRESOLVED)

- **Plane strain:** epsilon_zz = 0; can overconstrain lateral foam expansion and increase apparent stiffness.
- **Plane stress:** sigma_zz = 0; requires an appropriate finite-strain constitutive reduction and may permit lateral expansion but does not recover full 3D stress fields.
- **Generalized plane strain / calibrated reduction:** possible alternative if independent tests or a small 3D benchmark justify it.

The effective width does **not** decide among these. For initial verification, implement a linear-elastic plane-stress and plane-strain rectangle as separate reference benchmarks; choose the nonlinear foam reduction only after material/geometry review. A 2D model cannot predict medial-lateral COP, shoe torsion, or transverse plate bending.

### 15.4 Contact, ground motion, and coupling implications

The outsole-to-ground contact integration must use the same out-of-plane scaling convention as the continuum. Frictional tractions and normal contact tractions should be integrated consistently to total forces and sagittal moments. For treadmill simulations, compute slip relative to the belt velocity, not relative to the laboratory frame. For a stationary floor, belt velocity is zero.

Shoe FE inertia must be included exactly once in whole-system momentum. The provisional preferred fully coupled implementation remains floating-base multibody coordinates plus independent FE coordinates with explicit foot–shoe attachment constraints, not a six-state human-only COM integrator with shoe mass appended afterward. Revisit the exact total-COM formulation only after a consistent reduction is derived.

### 15.5 Required verification tests

1. **Width scaling:** For a linear elastic benchmark under prescribed displacement, doubling `b_eff` doubles total reaction, mass, and assembled stiffness while leaving displacement unchanged (when no competing fixed total loads are imposed).
2. **Unit bookkeeping:** Verify per-width and total force, moment, mass, and inertia conversion; test that the solver thickness is applied exactly once.
3. **Beam coupling:** Verify whole-plate `EI` is not multiplied by `b_eff`, and that the interface conserves force, moment, and work.
4. **Material reduction:** Compare plane-stress and plane-strain compression predictions against analytical small-strain solutions and independent material tests.
5. **Contact:** Verify outsole normal and tangential force integration, including belt-relative slip and frictional dissipation.
6. **Mesh/time convergence:** Verify force waveforms and deformation energy under mesh refinement and time-step reduction; quantify 2D reduction error with a small 3D benchmark when feasible.
7. **Parameter sensitivity:** Sweep independently justified `b_eff` and constitutive parameters, keeping measured validation GRFs out of optimization.

### 16.2 Variable-width continuum equations and numerical convention

Let `X` denote the **reference-configuration** heel-to-toe coordinate and `b(X) > 0` an independently specified effective out-of-plane width in meters. Let `Omega_0` be the reference sagittal cross-section, `rho_0` reference volumetric density, `u` the in-plane displacement field, `F` the deformation gradient, and `Psi(F, alpha)` the stored energy density per reference volume (with material internal variables `alpha`). Under the chosen and separately documented 2D constitutive reduction:

    U_foam = integral_Omega0 b(X) Psi(F, alpha) dA
    T_foam = 1/2 integral_Omega0 rho_0 b(X) |u_dot|^2 dA
    M_ij = integral_Omega0 rho_0 b(X) N_i N_j dA
    f_int,i = integral_Omega0 b(X) [B_i : P] dA

`P` and `B_i` must be defined consistently with the finite-strain weak form. The above expressions are schematic and do not by themselves choose a plane-stress or plane-strain constitutive model. Viscous and internal-variable contributions require the same width weighting. At each element quadrature point, evaluate `b(X_q)` in reference coordinates; never multiply already width-integrated total forces or masses by width again. Specify whether the selected FE framework treats a 2D problem as unit-thickness or allows variable thickness, and validate its actual assembly behavior.

For outsole boundary tractions, the physically correct reduced contact integral requires a separately justified *contact-width* convention. It may be approximated by `b(X)` initially, but the foam effective width and actual ground-contact width are not automatically identical. Contact forces, tangential friction, and contact moments must use one consistent total-force convention. Include the moving-belt velocity in treadmill slip kinematics.

**Physical caveat:** `b(X)` is a reduced-model weighting, not a complete representation of lateral foam bulging, edge effects, three-dimensional Poisson coupling, torsion, or spatially varying pressure across shoe width. Geometric outsole/foam width is a starting estimate, not proof of mechanical equivalence. A rapidly varying `b(X)` may require finer longitudinal mesh resolution; continuity or smooth interpolation must be documented.

### 16.3 Whole-plate beam convention

The beam represents the **entire carbon plate**, with sectional axial rigidity `EA(X)` in N, flexural rigidity `EI(X)` in N m^2, and line mass `mu(X)` in kg/m. These may vary along the plate and must be measured or independently inferred. Do **not** multiply these whole-plate properties by `b(X)`. If a library expects per-unit-width beam properties, convert exactly once and document the transformation. Beam-to-foam coupling must conserve equal-and-opposite interface forces and moments and must not double-count the plate mass. The beam theory (Euler–Bernoulli versus shear-deformable Timoshenko), finite rotation formulation, plate curvature, and bond/embedding formulation remain **OPEN**.

### 16.4 Required verification gates

1. **Constant-width recovery:** Setting `b(X)=b0` must recover the Section 15 constant-width benchmark for mass, stiffness, forces, and moments.
2. **Variable-width analytic check:** For a small-strain axial strip with known variable area, compare FE extension against `delta = integral F/[E A(X)] dX` with a clearly specified plane reduction and boundary conditions.
3. **Quadrature/mesh convergence:** Confirm that variable width is integrated at quadrature points and that reaction, total mass, strain energy, and displacement converge with mesh refinement.
4. **Unit consistency:** Verify `rho_0 b(X) dA` produces kg, internal-force assembly produces N, and external moment assembly produces N m; verify no duplicate thickness multiplier.
5. **Whole-plate beam check:** Recover analytic beam bending and axial benchmarks using whole-plate `EI`, `EA`, and line mass independent of foam width.
6. **Interface action–reaction:** Test force, moment, work, and momentum transfer between beam, foam, foot attachment, and ground.
7. **Independent geometry check:** Compare the integrated reduced foam mass `integral rho_0 b(X) dA` with measured shoe-component mass, while excluding plate/outsole masses counted elsewhere.
8. **Constitutive sensitivity:** Keep both plane-stress and plane-strain small-strain verification models available. Do not select the final nonlinear reduction by fitting held-out GRFs.

## 19. Initial state-free foam dissipation — decision update (draft 0.9)

### 19.1 Approved choice (2026-11-04)

**D24 — APPROVED:** Begin with a state-free, rate-dependent viscous stress contribution to the foam's elastic stress, rather than fractional viscoelasticity or generalized Maxwell/Prony branches. Motivation: limited independent data for identifying many viscous parameters and the desire to avoid additional constitutive memory states in periodic shooting. This is an **initial implementation decision**, not evidence that state-free viscosity reproduces the shoe's complete relaxation, hysteresis, or metabolic effects.

**Still OPEN:** the nonlinear elastic strain-energy function, objective finite-strain viscous stress law, number and sharing of viscosity parameters across distinct foam regions, plane-stress/plane-strain reduction, and final parameter values. Multiple foam materials remain approved; one common damping parameter is a possible low-parameter *candidate*, not an approved assumption.

### 19.2 Provisional candidate constitutive law (not yet approved)

In reference variables, define `F = I + Grad_X(u)`, `C = F.T @ F`, `E = (C-I)/2`, and `E_dot = (F_dot.T @ F + F.T @ F_dot)/2`. A simple objective, nonnegative-dissipation example is

```
P = dPsi(F, material_region)/dF + F @ S_visc
S_visc = 2 * eta * E_dot,   eta >= 0
D_visc = S_visc : E_dot = 2 * eta * (E_dot : E_dot) >= 0
```

Here `P` is first Piola–Kirchhoff stress and `S_visc` is second Piola–Kirchhoff stress. This is a **benchmark candidate**, not a universal foam law; large-compression behavior, volumetric viscosity, dimensional reduction, and material frame conventions must be checked. A scalar `eta` is only an initial parsimonious option. A more appropriate positive-semidefinite tensor viscosity may be necessary after independent shear/compression tests. Do not substitute Rayleigh/global numerical damping for physical material dissipation without a separate justification.

### 19.3 Consequences for FE states and shooting

- State-free viscosity requires current deformation and deformation rate, so it adds **no independent constitutive-history variables** at integration points.
- The dynamic FE model **still** contains nodal displacements and velocities, plus global floating-body states; these remain part of the periodic boundary-value problem. State-free viscosity does not make full FE shooting low-dimensional.
- A passive shoe can dissipate energy while returning to periodic FE displacement and velocity fields. Track `int P_visc : F_dot dV dt` consistently with the adopted reference formulation and report it separately from contact friction losses.
- Prescribed joint trajectories can supply mechanical work. Do not impose zero net joint work or equate shoe energy return directly to metabolic cost.
- Constitutive choice must not be tuned using held-out human force-plate GRFs.

### 19.4 Implementation and verification gates

1. Implement a purely elastic reference material and verify rigid-motion objectivity, zero stress for rigid rotations where appropriate, patch tests, and elastic energy conservation.
2. Add the viscous contribution as a separate, swappable constitutive component; test zero viscous stress at zero strain rate and frame invariance under superposed rigid rotations.
3. Verify nonnegative integrated viscous dissipation under synthetic cyclic compression and shear, and zero dissipation when `eta=0`.
4. Verify stress units, consistent 2D variable-width scaling, force/tangent derivatives, mesh and time-step convergence, and solver stability.
5. Fit a minimal number of viscosity parameters using independent material tests, then evaluate cyclic hysteresis and rate dependence on held-out *material* tests. Report uncertainty and failure to capture relaxation or long-memory effects.
6. Retain fractional and relaxation-branch laws as future alternatives only if independent measurements show systematic inadequacy of the state-free law.

### 19.5 Remaining decisions for the next review

- **M1:** Choose an initial elastic model for compressible foam (and specify which material tests can identify it).
- **M2:** Choose the viscous formulation: scalar objective finite-strain viscosity versus separate volumetric/deviatoric terms; decide whether parameters are per foam region or shared initially.
- **M3:** Choose plane stress, plane strain, or an alternative reduction based on independent material evidence.
- **M4:** Define material test conditions, fitting/held-out splits, and tolerances for hysteresis-loop area, force curves, and rate dependence.
- **M5:** Resolve foam–foam interface bonding, FE framework, beam–solid numerical coupling, boundary friction, and the coupled FE/multibody time integrator as previously listed in Section 18.

**Agent approval gate:** Agents may implement an isolated state-free viscous constitutive benchmark and its thermodynamic tests. They may not select a production elastic energy, assume a final viscosity parameterization, or use force-plate GRFs to identify material parameters without explicit scientific approval.

## 20. Decision update: hyperfoam elastic constitutive family (v1.0)

**D25 — APPROVED:** Use a **compressible hyperfoam-type finite-strain strain-energy potential** as the initial elastic constitutive family for each solid foam region, combined with the previously approved **state-free objective viscous stress** (D24). The hyperfoam family is approved; **no specific potential, parameter count, volumetric form, or coefficients have yet been approved**. This section supersedes earlier statements that the initial elastic *family* is undecided, but does not supersede open questions about plane stress versus plane strain, parameter identification, or numerical implementation.

### 20.1 Candidate mathematical form — provisional, not approved

A common compressible Ogden-type hyperfoam family is written in terms of the principal stretches `lambda_i` and volume ratio `J = lambda_1 lambda_2 lambda_3`:

`W = sum_{p=1}^N (2 mu_p / alpha_p^2) [lambda_1^alpha_p + lambda_2^alpha_p + lambda_3^alpha_p - 3 + (J^(-alpha_p beta_p) - 1)/beta_p]`.

This is an **illustrative parameterization only**; different FE packages use different hyperfoam definitions and parameter conventions. The limiting case `beta_p -> 0` requires a mathematically consistent evaluation. Before coding, choose one published/solver-specific form, derive or verify its stress and consistent tangent, and document units, stability constraints, and the mapping between its parameters and independent test data. The volumetric penalty must be consistent with foam compressibility and lateral deformation. The model must support multiple foam regions with independent parameter sets.

### 20.2 Dimensional reduction and width

The approved measured longitudinal midsole width `b(X)` multiplies the **reference-volume** energy, inertia, and weak-form contributions at quadrature points. The 2D model still requires an explicit out-of-plane closure. **Plane stress, plane strain, or another reduction remains OPEN.** In particular, plane stress for a compressible hyperfoam requires solving for the out-of-plane stretch so that the transverse stress vanishes; it is not obtained merely by deleting the transverse stretch term. The 2D effective-width approximation must be tested against independent mechanical measurements and eventually 3D benchmarks. Do not double-count width in whole-plate beam stiffness `EI` or mass per length.

### 20.3 Dissipation and energy

The elastic hyperfoam component derives from stored energy `W(F)` and must contribute **zero irreversible dissipation**. The separately implemented viscous component must be objective and have nonnegative dissipation under the adopted stress/strain-rate pairing. No additional material-memory variables are introduced initially. FE nodal displacements and velocities remain dynamic states and must still satisfy periodicity (or a documented approximate periodic residual). State-free viscosity may fail to reproduce relaxation and delayed recovery; assess that limitation on held-out material tests.

### 20.4 Verification and material identification

1. Implement the hyperfoam material as a standalone constitutive module before coupling to contact or OpenSim.
2. Test undeformed zero stress, rigid-body rotation objectivity, principal-stretch responses, compressive/tensile/shear paths, and consistent tangent against numerical differentiation.
3. Verify energy-stress consistency (`P = dW/dF`), finite-strain stability in the expected deformation range, and positive Jacobian `J` throughout valid simulations.
4. Compare one-term versus higher-order candidate potentials using independent compression, lateral strain/volume change, and shear measurements. Select model complexity based on held-out material data, not running GRFs.
5. Verify width-weighted force, mass, and energy assembly, and perfectly bonded beam–foam patch tests.
6. With state-free damping enabled, test passive cyclic energy loss, rate sensitivity, and convergence of time integration.

### 20.5 Remaining decisions and approval gates

- **H1 — Hyperfoam parameterization:** Which exact published/FE-solver strain-energy form and how many terms (`N`)? Provisional recommendation: start with one term, escalate only if independent material data require it.

6. **Zero-viscosity limit:** Recover purely elastic, reversible cycles and zero viscous dissipation.
7. **Viscous passivity:** Demonstrate nonnegative dissipation under cyclic compression and shear for the chosen stress-rate pairing.
8. **Independent data holdout:** Fit constitutive parameters only on material tests, then evaluate withheld loading amplitudes, rates and shear/compression paths; report force error and energy-loss error.
9. **Width scaling and multi-material tests:** Confirm variable-width quadrature and independent material regions reproduce analytically tractable benchmark results.

### 21.4 Next scientific decisions, ordered by implementation impact

### 22.1 Newly approved decisions

- **D28 — APPROVED: Plane strain.** Use a sagittal-plane **plane-strain** foam formulation initially: the out-of-plane displacement gradient components are zero and `F_33 = 1`, with nonzero transverse stress allowed. Do not set transverse stress to zero. Record that this is a 2D approximation requiring independent sensitivity checks because real foam can expand laterally. The measured longitudinal midsole width `b(X)` still weights reference-volume integrals and consistent inertia/contact calculations; plane strain does not make that width unnecessary.
- **D29 — APPROVED: Simple state-free viscosity parameterization.** Use **one independently identified, nonnegative viscosity coefficient per distinct foam material** in the initial implementation. The viscous stress depends on instantaneous deformation and deformation rate, with no additional constitutive history states. ### 22.2 Current constitutive architecture

At a material integration point, the initial stress is

`P(F,F_dot) = dW_Ogden-hyperfoam,N=1(F; mu, alpha, beta)/dF + P_viscous(F,F_dot; eta)`.

The symbols `(mu, alpha, beta)` denote *candidate* one-term parameter names, not a frozen convention or fitted values. The reference-state energy and stress must be normalized consistently, with `J = det(F) > 0`. Under plane strain, evaluate the full 3D constitutive potential on the embedded deformation gradient, including `F_33 = 1`, before reducing the in-plane residual and tangent. Transverse stress is generally nonzero. For multiple foam regions, use separate elastic parameters and one viscosity coefficient for each material. The variable geometric width `b(X)` is taken from measured midsole width, and the whole-plate beam properties remain whole-plate quantities (no second width multiplication).

**Energy requirement:** elastic stress derives from a potential and has no irreversible dissipation; the selected viscous law must be objective and satisfy nonnegative viscous power for all admissible deformations and rates. Its zero-viscosity limit must recover the purely hyperelastic model. A state-free viscous law need not reproduce stress relaxation at constant strain; this limitation must be assessed using independent tests rather than concealed by running-GRF fitting.

### 22.3 Verification tests now required

1. **Plane-strain homogeneous patch tests:** impose uniform axial compression, extension, and shear with `F_33 = 1`; compare FE forces against derivatives of the chosen 3D potential, including nonzero transverse stress.
2. **Energy, stress, tangent:** verify stress by finite differences of stored energy and the algorithmic tangent by independent differentiation across large compressions and shear, avoiding `J <= 0`.
3. **Objectivity and passivity:** apply superposed rigid rotations and closed cyclic deformation paths; verify elastic frame indifference and nonnegative viscous dissipation.
4. **One-viscosity identifiability:** test rate sensitivity on independent compression and shear histories and report systematic misfit rather than adding parameters without approval.
5. **Variable-width consistency:** verify force, mass, kinetic energy, and contact quadrature scaling under constant and varying `b(X)`; prevent double scaling of the plate.
6. **Material interfaces:** verify independent material properties per region; foam–foam bonding remains undecided, while plate–foam bonding is approved.
7. **Sensitivity to 2D closure:** compare plane-strain predictions with plane-stress or 3D benchmarks and independent lateral-strain measurements before claiming quantitative shoe-force accuracy.

## 24. Decision update — published hyperfoam and bonded foam interfaces (v1.3, 2026-11-04)

### 24.1 Approved decisions

- **Published hyperfoam potential:** use the one-term compressible Ogden/Storakers-type **hyperfoam** energy, not the conventional nearly incompressible Ogden rubber model. Adopt the Abaqus-style parameter convention below as the proposed *exact documented reference expression*; coefficient values remain to be identified from independent material tests.
- **Foam–foam interfaces:** perfect bonding in the initial model. Adjacent foam regions share compatible displacement/velocity fields; the interface transmits traction and does not permit separation or tangential slip. If meshes are conforming, use shared nodes/continuous displacement fields. If nonconforming, use a verified tie or mortar constraint. Preserve distinct material properties on either side.
- Retain earlier approvals: 2D plane strain, variable midsole width b(X), multiple foam materials, bonded carbon-plate beam, boundary ground contact, state-free viscous dissipation, and independent GRF validation.

### 24.2 Published one-term hyperfoam energy and conventions

Use reference-volume energy density (J > 0):

    W(F) = (2*mu/alpha**2) * [lambda1**alpha + lambda2**alpha
            + lambda3**alpha - 3 + (J**(-alpha*beta)-1)/beta]
    J = det(F) = lambda1*lambda2*lambda3

where lambda1, lambda2, lambda3 are the **full principal stretches** (not isochoric stretches); mu [Pa] is the initial shear modulus in this normalization, alpha [-] is the nonlinear exponent, and beta [-] controls compressibility. The beta -> 0 limit must be evaluated continuously as -alpha*ln(J). For plane strain set F33=1 and out-of-plane shear components to zero; retain the three-dimensional constitutive energy before restricting kinematics. The out-of-plane stress generally need not vanish.

This convention is documented by the Abaqus hyperfoam material fitting reference (https://docs.software.vt.edu/abaqusv2025/English/SIMACAETHERefMap/simathe-c-fithyperconst.htm) and matches the one-term form in the cited published literature. **Do not interchange** coefficients with the alternative ANSYS normalization without an explicit conversion. For this convention the small-strain bulk modulus is K = 2*mu*(beta + 1/3); physical small-strain stability requires mu > 0 and beta > -1/3, with further admissibility/stability checks across the actual strain range. Check the parameter interpretation against the reference source before coding.

For spatially variable midsole width, integrate b(X)*W(F) over the reference sagittal domain; use the same width convention for mass, inertia, viscous internal forces, and boundary contact. Whole-plate EI and EA are total sectional properties and must not be multiplied by b(X) again.

### 24.3 Recommended (not approved) state-free viscous stress

A candidate objective, passive finite-strain Kelvin–Voigt contribution is:

    C = F.T @ F
    E = 0.5*(C - I)
    E_dot = 0.5*(F_dot.T @ F + F.T @ F_dot)
    S_visc = 2*eta*E_dot
    P_visc = F @ S_visc
    D = S_visc : E_dot = 2*eta*(E_dot : E_dot) >= 0  for eta >= 0.

Here eta has units Pa*s. This is a **candidate**, not an approved constitutive equation. It is frame indifferent under superposed rigid rotations and uses no history states beyond nodal displacement and velocity. Its large-strain behavior and implied volumetric/shear dissipation coupling must be tested against compression and shear data; one eta per foam material is approved for the initial model. Avoid interpreting a state-free rate law as a general stress-relaxation model.

### 24.4 Recommended (not approved) FE software architecture

- **First framework to benchmark:** FEniCSx/DOLFINx with UFL automatic differentiation and PETSc nonlinear solvers; Python orchestration. This is not yet a final software selection.
- **Benchmarks before commitment:** (1) plane-strain hyperfoam patch test, (2) tangent-vs-finite-difference test, (3) objective viscous dissipation test, (4) two-region bonded-material interface test, (5) variable-width force/mass scaling, (6) beam-to-solid force and moment transfer, (7) unilateral boundary contact and friction, (8) dynamic implicit step with momentum and energy audits.
- **Major risks:** embedded beam coupling, contact/friction algorithms, high-dimensional FE periodicity, and nonlinear time integration are not turnkey just because a library supports hyperelasticity. Assess development cost and solver convergence before final commitment. Keep core multibody and contact interfaces independent of FE framework.
- **Provisional integrator:** implicit generalized-alpha or Newmark-type method, with consistent tangent and contact-event checks. Solver tolerances and timestep convergence must be established empirically.

### 24.5 Verification and identification gates

1. Verify W(I)=0, P_elastic(I)=0, material frame indifference, stress from energy differentiation, and tangent consistency.
2. Verify plane strain retains nonzero sigma33 where appropriate; do not substitute plane stress accidentally.
3. Verify each material region's parameters and interface tractions separately; perfect bonding must not force material parameters to be equal.
4. Verify zero viscous stress at zero strain rate, nonnegative viscous dissipation, and zero viscous response under superposed rigid rotation.
5. Fit mu, alpha, beta and eta only to independent foam characterization data. With limited data, report identifiability and uncertainty; do not fit held-out human GRFs.
6. Check compression stability, near-collapse behavior, mesh convergence, and sensitivity to the plane-strain approximation before proceeding to running simulations.

### 24.6 Remaining decisions, ordered by priority

1. **FE framework:** confirm FEniCSx/PETSc after the benchmark suite, or select an alternative if embedded-beam/contact implementation proves disproportionate.
2. **Viscous stress law:** approve or revise the Green–Lagrange-rate formulation above after large-compression/shear tests.
3. **Contact:** specify normal enforcement (penalty/augmented Lagrangian/complementarity), friction regularization, and stationary/moving ground treatment.
4. **Beam-to-solid bond:** select a finite-rotation-consistent embedded beam formulation and verify moments and energy transfer.
5. **Dynamic coupling:** monolithic versus partitioned body–FE solve, integration scheme, and stability/convergence criteria.
6. **Periodic FE states:** shooting versus multiple shooting or reduced-order initialization; FE displacement/velocity states must be treated consistently even without constitutive history states.
7. **Material characterization:** select specimen tests, preconditioning, strain-rate range, and identification/holdout protocol for each foam region.

### 25.2 Approved viscous stress equation and verification

For deformation gradient F, right Cauchy–Green tensor C=F^T F, and Green–Lagrange strain E=(C-I)/2, define E_dot=(F_dot^T F+F^T F_dot)/2. For each material region with eta >= 0:

    S_visc = 2 eta E_dot
    P_visc = F S_visc
    D_visc = S_visc:E_dot = 2 eta (E_dot:E_dot) >= 0

Here S_visc is second Piola–Kirchhoff stress, P_visc is first Piola–Kirchhoff stress, eta is in Pa*s, and the dot denotes a material time derivative. Implement in a consistent total-Lagrangian formulation; verify zero response to superposed rigid rotation and positive dissipation in compression and shear. The law is an **initial approximation**, not an assertion that it captures foam relaxation or broad-frequency behavior. Finite-strain hyperfoam stress and viscous stress must be added using consistent stress measures. Verify large-compression stability.

### 25.3 Approved normal-contact architecture and caveats

At the shoe-ground boundary use a gap g_n (positive when separated), penetration delta=max(0,-g_n), and a smooth, nonnegative penalty traction. A candidate baseline is t_n=k_n * smooth_positive(-g_n), with smooth_positive chosen to approach zero in separation; an optional dissipative normal-rate contribution must be separately justified and must not generate tensile traction. The contact law must be integrated along the actual contact boundary with appropriate width factors; use ground-relative velocity for treadmill cases. **The specific smoothing function, penalty stiffness selection, contact quadrature, and tangential friction law remain provisional.** Record the numerical penetration error and sensitivity to k_n, smoothing length, and mesh/time step. The numerical penalty stiffness is a constraint-enforcement parameter, distinct from the physical hyperfoam stiffness; never use it to compensate for errors in the foam law.
